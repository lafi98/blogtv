If you have ever followed a crochet pattern carefully and ended up with something far taller than the photo, mismatched terminology may be the reason. US and UK patterns use the same stitches, but several of them go by different names, and some names, like "double crochet", appear in both systems for different stitches.

This guide is for anyone who uses patterns from more than one source. You will find a full conversion table, ways to tell which terms a pattern uses, the other vocabulary that differs, how hook sizes are named, and a step-by-step method for converting a pattern safely. Elsewhere on this site, we use US terms.

## The short explanation

The stitches are identical; only the names shift. A handy way to remember it is that the UK name is the US name moved one step up the ladder:

- US single crochet → UK double crochet
- US half double crochet → UK half treble
- US double crochet → UK treble
- US treble crochet → UK double treble

Chain and slip stitch are the same in both systems. Modern UK terminology has no "single crochet" at all, which turns out to be a useful clue.

## Full conversion table

| US term | US abbr. | UK term | UK abbr. |
|---|---|---|---|
| chain | ch | chain | ch |
| slip stitch | sl st | slip stitch | sl st or ss |
| single crochet | sc | double crochet | dc |
| half double crochet | hdc | half treble crochet | htr |
| double crochet | dc | treble crochet | tr |
| treble (triple) crochet | tr | double treble crochet | dtr |
| double treble crochet | dtr | triple treble crochet | trtr or ttr |

If you are reading a UK pattern and need the US name, read the same table from right to left: UK dc is US sc, UK htr is US hdc, UK tr is US dc, UK dtr is US tr, and UK triple treble is US dtr.

Stitches built on these names convert the same way. For example, the US decrease sc2tog (single crochet two together) is dc2tog in UK terms, and a US front post double crochet (FPdc) is a UK front post treble (FPtr).

## The classic trap: "double crochet"

"Double crochet" and its abbreviation "dc" exist in both systems, but they mean different stitches.

**US double crochet** is a tall stitch:

1. Yarn over, then insert the hook.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (3 loops on hook).
3. Yarn over and pull through 2 loops, then yarn over and pull through the last 2.

**UK double crochet** is a short stitch, the one US patterns call single crochet:

1. Insert the hook, with no yarn over first.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (2 loops on hook).
3. Yarn over and pull through both loops.

If you read a UK pattern as though it were written in US terms, every "dc" becomes a much taller stitch. The project comes out bigger and looser than intended, uses more yarn, and will not match the pattern's gauge. Making the opposite mistake gives a small, stiff piece. In a [granny square](/blog/classic-granny-square-pattern), for instance, the familiar clusters of three US double crochet appear as clusters of three trebles in a UK pattern.

"Treble" (tr) is the same kind of trap: a US treble starts with two yarn overs, while a UK treble starts with one. Even "dtr" means a different stitch in each system. The names that appear in only one system are the most reliable clues: sc and hdc are US-only, and htr is UK-only.

## How to tell which terms a pattern uses

1. **Look for a note.** Many patterns state "US terms" or "UK terms" near the materials list or abbreviations.
2. **Scan the abbreviations.** If you see sc or hdc, the pattern uses US terms. If you see htr, it uses UK terms.
3. **Read the stitch guide.** If the pattern explains "dc" as insert hook, yarn over and pull up a loop, then yarn over and pull through 2 loops, it is a UK pattern.
4. **Check the turning chains.** A line such as "ch 1, dc in each st" pairs a short turning chain with dc, which suggests UK terms, because a single chain suits a short stitch. "Ch 3 (counts as tr)" points the same way.
5. **Notice other vocabulary.** Words such as "tension", "miss" and "yrh" suggest UK terms. Treat these, and spellings such as "colour", as hints rather than proof, since some designers mix conventions.
6. **Look at the hook size.** Sizes given only as letters, such as H-8, suggest a US pattern. Millimeters are used in both.
7. **Compare with the photo.** If the photo shows dense, solid fabric but your version is tall and open, the terms may be mismatched. A small test swatch settles the question quickly.

Where you found a pattern is not a reliable guide on its own. Designers in many countries write in either system, and UK terms are used in a number of places beyond the UK. Vintage patterns can follow older conventions that do not match either modern system exactly, so read their stitch explanations closely.

## Other vocabulary differences

| US | UK | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| gauge | tension | the number of stitches and rows in a measured area |
| skip | miss | pass over a stitch without working into it |
| yarn over (yo) | yarn round hook (yrh) or yarn over hook (yoh) | wrap the yarn over the hook |
| work even | work straight | continue without increasing or decreasing |

One nuance: in US usage, "tension" usually describes how firmly you hold and feed your yarn. In a UK pattern, a section headed "tension" gives the gauge.

Yarn weight names differ as well. These matches are approximate:

| US name | Approximate UK name |
|---|---|
| fingering | 4 ply |
| light worsted or DK | double knitting (DK) |
| worsted | aran (often a little thicker) |
| bulky | chunky |
| super bulky | super chunky |

Because these names do not line up perfectly, compare the length per ball weight and the suggested hook size on each label. The numbered [standard yarn weight system](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system) is a helpful common reference.

## Hook sizes have different names too

US hooks carry a letter and a number, such as H-8 for 5 mm. In the UK and many other countries, hooks are labeled in millimeters. Older UK hooks, which still turn up in inherited and secondhand collections, used a numbering system in which a larger number meant a smaller hook:

| Metric | US | Old UK |
|---|---|---|
| 4 mm | G-6 | 8 |
| 4.5 mm | 7 | 7 |
| 5 mm | H-8 | 6 |
| 5.5 mm | I-9 | 5 |
| 6 mm | J-10 | 4 |

Notice that a US H-8 and an old UK size 8 are different hooks: 5 mm and 4 mm. Millimeters are always the safest way to compare. The Craft Yarn Council's [hook size chart](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/hooks-and-needles) lists the full range of metric and US sizes.

## What you'll need

To convert a pattern, gather:

- The pattern, printed or saved as a file you can annotate
- A pencil or highlighter
- The conversion table above
- The hook and yarn you plan to use, for a quick test swatch

## How to convert a pattern safely

1. **Identify the system first.** Use the clues above before you make a single stitch.
2. **Work on a copy.** Write the converted stitch name above or beside each original rather than replacing it, so you can always check back against the source.
3. **Avoid blind find-and-replace.** Swapping terms automatically can convert the same stitch twice. For example, changing UK "tr" to "dc" and then changing every "dc" to "sc" would turn each treble into a single crochet instead of a double crochet.
4. **Convert related stitches too.** Decreases, post stitches, clusters and special stitches named after basic stitches all shift in the same way.
5. **Leave the numbers alone.** Chain lengths, stitch counts, repeats and measurements stay the same. Only the stitch names change.
6. **Make a gauge swatch.** Working a small sample and measuring it confirms that you are making the right stitches at the right size. Our guide to [crochet gauge](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide) explains how.
7. **Check stitch counts as you go.** If your counts match the pattern at the end of each row or round, your conversion is on track.

## Tips

- Keep a small conversion card with your crochet supplies until the names become second nature.
- Label your own notes "US" or "UK" at the top so you never have to guess later.
- If a pattern uses a stitch you do not recognize, look for a stitch guide or special stitches section before converting it.
- When in doubt, try both readings on a small swatch and compare them with the pattern photo.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

**Assuming the terms from the designer's location or the website.** Always look for a note, or for clues in the pattern itself.

**Converting dc but forgetting tr.** Both stitch names change. Go through the pattern once for each stitch type.

**Matching hooks by letter or old number.** Compare hooks by millimeters, especially when using older UK hooks.

**Mixing systems in one project.** If you combine motifs or edgings from different patterns, convert them all into one system before you start.
