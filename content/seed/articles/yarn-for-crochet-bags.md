A bag asks more of yarn than a scarf or blanket does. It has to carry weight without stretching, hold its shape, survive being scraped against counters and car seats, and ideally come clean when something leaks. The fiber you choose, and the way you crochet it, decide how well a bag manages all of that.

This guide walks through the common bag yarns, what each one does well and where it struggles, and how hook size, stitch choice, handle reinforcement and linings make any of them more dependable. It's written for anyone choosing yarn for a first bag, and for anyone whose last tote ended up much longer than planned.

## What makes a good bag yarn

Before comparing fibers, it helps to know what you're looking for:

- **Low stretch.** Yarn that doesn't elongate under load keeps handles and bodies the length you crocheted them.
- **Strength and abrasion resistance.** Bags rub against everything, so a smooth, tightly twisted yarn that doesn't fuzz quickly will look better for longer.
- **Reasonable weight.** Some fibers make a heavy bag before you've put anything in it.
- **Structure.** Crisp fibers help a bag stand up on its own; soft fibers make slouchy bags.
- **Practical care.** Think about whether you'll want to machine wash it. Your yarn label lists fiber content and care symbols, and the guide on [how to read a yarn label](/blog/how-to-read-a-yarn-label) explains what they mean.

## Bag yarns, fiber by fiber

### Cotton

Cotton is the most common bag yarn for good reason. It's strong, has very little stretch, shows stitch patterns clearly and generally washes well, though it can shrink a little in hot water or a hot dryer. It's also fairly heavy, and because it doesn't spring back once stretched, a loosely crocheted cotton bag can slowly lengthen under heavy loads.

Mercerized cotton has been treated to make it smoother, shinier and stronger. It resists fuzzing and gives crisp, defined stitches, which suits clutches and structured bags. Unmercerized cotton is softer and matte, with a more casual look.

### Cotton blends

Blending cotton with another fiber changes its behavior. Cotton mixed with acrylic or polyester is usually lighter and a little more elastic, so it sags less under its own weight. Cotton and linen together feel crisp and textured. Blends that include viscose (including bamboo-based viscose) have more drape and sheen, which suits slouchy shoulder bags more than structured ones. Blends vary widely, so follow the care instructions on the label.

### Linen and hemp

Linen and hemp are plant fibers known for strength and almost no stretch, which makes them excellent for market bags. Linen is also noted for being stronger wet than dry. Both can feel stiff and slightly rough at first, but they soften with washing and use. The fabric is crisp and lightweight in mesh designs, though it can feel harsh to crochet with.

### Jute

Jute is coarse, stiff and rustic. That stiffness gives great structure for baskets and structured totes, but it's scratchy to handle, tends to shed fibers, and doesn't take well to soaking. It can develop mildew if it stays damp, so plan on spot cleaning.

### Raffia-style paper yarn

Paper yarns are made from twisted or flattened paper and are popular for summer bags. They're very light and crisp, holding their shape well in clutches and structured totes. Water is their weak point: soaking can soften or weaken paper, so most need spot cleaning only. Check the label, as some raffia-style yarns are made from other materials with different care needs.

### T-shirt yarn

T-shirt yarn is made from strips of stretchy knit fabric, much like the material of a T-shirt. It's very bulky and works up fast on a large hook, often 10 mm (US N/P-15) or bigger depending on the strip width. It's also heavy and stretchy, so bags made with it lengthen under load unless worked tightly. Thickness can vary between balls, especially in yarn made from recycled fabric. It's well suited to baskets, sturdy bag bases and chunky totes for light loads.

### Acrylic

Acrylic is lightweight, easy to care for and comes in a huge color range. On the downside, it stretches under weight, can pill or fuzz where it rubs, and doesn't tolerate high heat well, so avoid hot irons and high dryer settings. It's a good fit for small bags, kids' bags and lined clutches, but less so for heavy shopping.

## Structure comes from your stitches too

### Tighter gauge, smaller hook

The same yarn can make a floppy bag or a firm one, depending on your hook size and gauge (UK tension). A smaller hook makes shorter, tighter stitches with less room to shift, so the fabric stretches less, holds its shape and keeps small items from poking through. If a worsted yarn's label suggests 5 mm (US H-8), try 4.5 mm (US 7) or 4 mm (US G-6) for a bag. The Craft Yarn Council's [hook size chart](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/hooks-and-needles) lists metric and US equivalents.

The trade-offs are real: tighter fabric uses more yarn, weighs more and takes more effort to crochet. Make a swatch, tug it in both directions and see how much it gives. The [gauge swatch guide](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide) explains how to make and measure one.

### Dense stitches vs. mesh

Dense stitches such as single crochet (UK double crochet), waistcoat stitch and moss stitch make solid fabric that's ideal for bases, clutches and crossbody bags. Half double crochet (UK half treble crochet) is a little looser but still sturdy. If any of these are new to you, start with the [basic crochet stitches](/blog/basic-crochet-stitches).

Mesh, made from chain spaces anchored by stitches, is light and expands to hold bulky shopping. It also stretches a lot lengthwise, so pair it with a low-stretch fiber like cotton or linen, and give it a dense base, top band and handles.

### Holding yarn double

Working with two strands held together creates thicker, denser and stronger fabric. As a rough guide, two strands of worsted behave somewhat like one strand of bulky yarn; the Craft Yarn Council's [yarn weight system](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system) shows how the categories compare. Choose a hook to suit the combined thickness and swatch to check. Doubling just the handles or base is a useful trick, even when the body is single-stranded.

## Reinforcing handles

Handles carry the whole load through just a few stitches, so they deserve extra attention:

- Crochet handles in dense single crochet, using a smaller hook than the body.
- Work single crochet over a length of cotton cord so the cord takes the strain.
- Sew a strip of cotton webbing along the inside of a crochet handle.
- Add a row of slip stitches along each long edge of a strap to limit stretch.
- Spread the attachment across several stitches and rows rather than anchoring at one point, and add a few dense rounds at the top of the bag first.
- Use ready-made wooden, bamboo or leather handles, stitched securely through several rows.

## Adding a fabric lining

A lining stops a bag from stretching, keeps small items from slipping through stitches and gives you a place to sew pockets.

1. Choose a woven fabric such as quilting cotton, canvas or twill, and pre-wash it so it won't shrink later.
2. Measure the bag lying flat and relaxed, without stretching it, and add a seam allowance.
3. Sew a simple bag shape from the fabric, boxing the corners if your bag has a flat base.
4. Fold the top edge under, pin the lining inside just below the rim, and hand-sew it in place with small stitches.

Sizing the lining to the relaxed bag means the fabric carries the load instead of the crochet. For a clutch, fusing interfacing to the lining fabric adds extra structure.

## Matching yarn to the bag

### Market tote

Market totes carry heavy, bulky loads and get washed often. Choose cotton, linen, hemp or a cotton-linen blend. A mesh body keeps the bag light, while a dense base and reinforced handles do the heavy lifting.

### Clutch

A clutch has no handles to strain, so appearance and structure matter most. Mercerized cotton, paper raffia or a cotton blend worked at a tight gauge in dense stitches look crisp, and a lining with interfacing keeps the shape sharp. Acrylic works well here too, since loads are light.

### Crossbody

A crossbody bag hangs its full weight from one narrow strap that rubs against clothing. Make the strap dense and low-stretch: cotton held double, single crochet worked over cord, or a ready-made strap. Cotton or a cotton blend in a medium weight keeps the body light, and a lining helps with keys and coins.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **Choosing a stretchy yarn for a heavy bag.** Acrylic and soft, loosely plied cottons can stretch under weight. Pick a firm cotton, linen or a sturdy blend, add a lining, or reinforce the handles.
- **Working at your usual tension.** Fabric made with the hook size printed on the label is often too loose for a bag. Go down a hook size or two and check a swatch before you start.
- **Skipping the swatch.** Fiber, stitch and tension all change how fabric behaves. A 4 in (10 cm) square you can tug, load and wash tells you more than the label does.
- **Attaching handles at a single point.** Spread each join across several stitches and rows so the weight isn't concentrated in one spot.
- **Washing jute or paper yarn like cotton.** Check the label first — many plant-fiber and paper yarns don't like soaking.

## Tips

- Buy all the yarn for one bag from the same dye lot, and keep a few yards back for future repairs.
- For a shopping bag that will carry heavy loads, combine a mesh body with a dense base and solid handles.
- Test a finished bag by hanging it up with a typical load inside (a few books or cans) for a while. If it stretches noticeably, add a lining or stiffer handles.

## Start small, then scale up

The quickest way to test a bag yarn is to crochet a swatch at your planned gauge, then pull it, wash it if you plan to and see how it behaves. A small project tells you even more: the [crochet drawstring pouch](/blog/crochet-drawstring-pouch-pattern) is a quick way to try a new fiber before committing to a full-size tote.
