Crochet patterns use a compact shorthand that can look like code at first. Once you know how a pattern is laid out and what its abbreviations and punctuation mean, most instructions turn out to be a plain list of steps.

This guide is for beginners who know a few stitches and are ready to follow a written pattern or a symbol chart. You'll learn what each section of a pattern is for, how repeats and stitch counts work, how to read a chart, and how to decode a full line of instructions.

## The parts of a crochet pattern

Most patterns follow a similar order. It's worth reading the whole pattern once before you pick up your hook.

### Skill level and finished size

Many patterns state a skill level, often beginner, easy, intermediate or experienced, and the finished measurements. If a pattern comes in several sizes, the numbers usually appear together, such as "ch 41 (45, 49)", smallest size first. Highlight the numbers for your size throughout before you start.

### Materials

This lists the yarn (weight, fiber, yards or meters needed, and colors), the hook size, and notions: small extras such as stitch markers, a tapestry needle, buttons or stuffing. If you plan to substitute a yarn, [reading the yarn label](/blog/how-to-read-a-yarn-label) helps you match its weight and quantity.

### Gauge

Gauge, called tension in UK patterns, tells you how many stitches and rows fit into a set measurement, usually 4 in (10 cm), with the stated hook. For anything that needs to fit, check yours first; [Crochet Gauge Explained](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide) shows you how.

### Notes

This section is easy to skip, but it explains things the instructions take for granted, such as whether the turning chain counts as a stitch, whether rounds are joined, and whether the pattern uses US or UK terms. Skipping it can throw off your counts for the whole project.

### Abbreviations and special stitches

Standard abbreviations are usually listed briefly. Special stitches, such as clusters or puffs, are spelled out in full because the method can vary between designers.

### Instructions and finishing

Then come the row-by-row or round-by-round instructions, followed by finishing: fastening off (cutting the yarn and pulling the end through the last loop), weaving in ends, assembly and sometimes blocking.

## Common crochet abbreviations

This guide uses US terms, with UK equivalents for the main stitches in parentheses. The same abbreviation can mean a different stitch in each system: "dc" is a double crochet in a US pattern but a shorter stitch (the US single crochet) in a UK one. That's why it pays to know the difference between [US and UK crochet terms](/blog/us-vs-uk-crochet-terms).

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ch | chain |
| sl st | slip stitch |
| sc | single crochet (UK double crochet) |
| hdc | half double crochet (UK half treble crochet) |
| dc | double crochet (UK treble crochet) |
| tr | treble crochet (UK double treble crochet) |
| st(s) | stitch(es) |
| sp | space, the gap under a chain (a "ch-2 sp" sits under 2 chains) |
| sk | skip (UK patterns often say "miss") |
| yo | yarn over (UK patterns may say "yarn round hook", yrh) |
| inc | increase, usually 2 stitches worked into 1 stitch |
| dec | decrease; the exact method is given in the pattern |
| sc2tog | single crochet 2 stitches together (a one-stitch decrease) |
| BLO / FLO | back loop only / front loop only |
| beg | beginning |
| rep | repeat |
| rnd(s) | round(s) |
| RS / WS | right side / wrong side |
| pm | place marker |
| tog | together |

BLO and FLO refer to the two strands that form the V on top of every stitch:

## Repeats: asterisks, brackets and parentheses

### Asterisks

An asterisk (\*) marks the start of a section you'll repeat. The words after the semicolon tell you how many times:

- **"rep from \* across"** or **"rep from \* to end":** keep repeating until you run out of stitches.
- **"rep from \* 3 more times":** work the section once as written, then 3 more times, for 4 in total. If a pattern just says "3 times", the stitch count usually shows which the designer meant.
- **"rep from \* to last 2 sts":** stop repeating when 2 stitches remain, then follow the instructions for those.
- **"rep from \* to \*\*":** some patterns use a double asterisk to mark where a partial repeat ends.

### Parentheses and brackets

Parentheses ( ) and square brackets [ ] each do several jobs, and designers don't all use them the same way:

- **Grouping stitches into one place.** "(2 dc, ch 2, 2 dc) in next ch-2 sp" means all of that goes into the same space.
- **Repeating a short sequence.** "[sc in next st, 2 sc in next st] 6 times" means work that pair of instructions six times in a row.
- **Sizes**, as in "ch 41 (45, 49)".
- **Stitch counts and notes**, such as "(18 sts)" or "(counts as first dc)".

When one set of brackets sits inside another, repeat the inner group within every repeat of the outer one.

### Stitch counts at the end of rows

Many rows and rounds end with a number, such as "(18 sts)" or "— 18 sc", telling you how many stitches you should have. These are your checkpoints. In the bracket example above, if you started with 12 stitches, each repeat uses 2 stitches and makes 3, so six repeats use all 12 and leave you with 18.

If your count is off, check the start and end of the row first, where stitches are most often gained or lost. [Why Your Crochet Edges Are Uneven](/blog/fix-uneven-crochet-edges) covers the usual culprits.

## Rows vs rounds

**Rows** are worked back and forth. At the end of each row you turn the work and usually make a turning chain, a short chain that brings your hook up to the height of the next row. The pattern notes tell you whether it counts as a stitch.

**Rounds** are worked in a circle or tube, starting from a ring or a chain. They may be joined with a slip stitch at the end of each round or worked in a continuous spiral, with a marker showing where each round begins. Many patterns don't turn between rounds, but some do, so follow your pattern.

## Reading crochet symbol charts

Some patterns include a chart, a drawing in which every stitch is shown as a symbol. Charts are especially handy for lace and motifs, because you can see how the stitches stack up.

![Crochet chart symbol key showing chain, slip stitch, single crochet, half double crochet, double crochet and treble crochet symbols](/images/diagrams/crochet-symbol-key.svg)

### The basic symbols

| Symbol | Stitch |
|---|---|
| Oval | chain |
| Small filled dot | slip stitch |
| X or + | single crochet |
| T | half double crochet |
| T with one diagonal slash | double crochet |
| T with two diagonal slashes | treble crochet |

For taller stitches, the number of slashes across the stem matches the number of yarn overs you make before inserting your hook. Symbols vary slightly between publishers, so always check the chart's own key. The Craft Yarn Council also keeps a reference of [standard crochet chart symbols](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/crochet-chart-symbols).

### Charts worked in rows

Start at the bottom with the foundation chain and read upward. Each row's number sits at the end where that row begins. For right-handed crocheters, that usually means reading Row 1 from right to left, then switching direction every row because you turn the work. Left-handed crocheters read each row the opposite way.

### Charts worked in rounds

Start at the center, where the ring or starting chain is drawn, and read outward one round at a time. Right-handed crocheters read each round counterclockwise; left-handed crocheters read clockwise. Some charts print alternate rounds in different colors to make them easier to follow.

## A worked example: decoding one line

Here's a sample row written for this guide. Imagine you've just finished Row 3 with 31 single crochet.

> **Row 4:** Ch 1, turn. Sc in first st, \*sk next 2 sts, (dc, ch 1, dc) in next st, sk next 2 sts, sc in next st; rep from \* to end. (5 V-sts, 6 sc)

Piece by piece:

1. **Ch 1, turn.** Make one chain, then turn your work. Before single crochet, this chain usually doesn't count as a stitch.
2. **Sc in first st.** Work a single crochet into the very first stitch, right at the base of the chain.
3. **\*** The asterisk marks the start of the repeat.
4. **Sk next 2 sts.** Leave the next two stitches unworked.
5. **(dc, ch 1, dc) in next st.** Work a double crochet, a chain 1 and another double crochet, all into the same stitch. This group is often called a V-stitch.
6. **Sk next 2 sts, sc in next st.** Skip two more stitches, then single crochet in the next. That completes one repeat, which used 6 stitches from the row below.
7. **; rep from \* to end.** Go back to the asterisk and repeat steps 4 to 6 until you reach the end of the row.
8. **(5 V-sts, 6 sc).** The stitch count. The first sc used 1 stitch and each repeat used 6, so 1 + (5 × 6) = 31, exactly what you had. Each repeat made one V-stitch and one sc, giving 5 V-stitches and 1 + 5 = 6 sc.

Because this row needs groups of 6 plus 1 extra stitch, a pattern built on it might ask you to start with "a multiple of 6 sts plus 1".

## Tips

- **Print or copy the pattern and mark it up.** Tick off rows as you finish them and highlight your size.
- **Use stitch markers** at the start of rounds and every few repeats in long rows, so a miscount is easy to trace.
- **Read ahead when you're stuck.** Seeing what the next row works into can explain the one you're on.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **Following a UK pattern with US stitches.** If a pattern mentions single crochet (sc), it's in US terms, because UK terminology has no single crochet. If not, check the notes or look for UK clues such as "tension", "miss" or "yrh".
- **Miscounting repeats.** "Rep 3 more times" means 4 in total. Use the stitch count to confirm.
- **Working a group into the wrong place.** "In next st" and "in next sp" are different targets.
- **Waiting until the end to count.** Check at each stitch count given, so you only ever have a row or two to undo.

## Where to go from here

The more patterns you read, the quicker the shorthand becomes. If any stitch above is unfamiliar, [The Basic Crochet Stitches](/blog/basic-crochet-stitches) covers each one step by step.
