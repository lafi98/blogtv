If you have never held a crochet hook, this lesson is the place to start. It walks you through the handful of moves that almost every crochet project is built on: making a slip knot, holding the hook and yarn, crocheting a chain, working single crochet into that chain, turning, counting, fastening off and weaving in your yarn ends.

By the end you will have a small practice swatch, a little square of fabric made purely for learning. It does not need to look perfect. Its job is to get your hands familiar with the motions. The steps use US crochet terms and describe right-handed crocheting; if you are left-handed, hold the hook in your left hand and mirror each step.

## What you'll need

- A crochet hook in 5 mm (US H-8) or 5.5 mm (US I-9)
- Smooth, light-colored medium-weight yarn (often labeled worsted or #4), such as acrylic or cotton
- Small scissors
- A tapestry needle (a blunt needle with an eye large enough for yarn)
- One or two removable stitch markers (optional but helpful)

If you are still gathering supplies, our guide to [what you need to start crocheting](/blog/crochet-supplies-for-beginners) explains how to choose each item.

## A few terms to know first

- **Working yarn:** the yarn that leads back to the ball.
- **Tail:** the loose cut end of the yarn.
- **Yarn over (yo):** wrapping the working yarn over the hook from back to front, so the hook can catch it.
- **Loop on the hook:** the loop that stays on your hook between stitches. It never counts as a stitch.

## Step 1: Make a slip knot

The slip knot attaches the yarn to your hook. It is not counted as a stitch.

1. Leaving a tail of about 6 in (15 cm), make a loop by crossing the working yarn over the tail.
2. Push the hook (or your fingers) through the loop, catch the working yarn, and pull a new loop through.
3. Place that new loop on the hook and pull gently on both ends until it sits snugly around the hook. It should be firm but still able to slide.

## Step 2: Hold the hook and yarn

### Holding the hook

There are two common grips. In the **pencil grip**, you hold the hook the way you would hold a pencil. In the **knife grip**, your hand rests over the top of the hook, as if holding a dinner knife. Neither is more correct, so choose whichever feels natural, with your thumb and index finger on the flat thumb rest.

### Holding the yarn

Your other hand controls the yarn and holds the work. A common approach is to drape the working yarn over your index finger, let it run down across your palm, and wrap it loosely around your little finger to add a little resistance. Then pinch the work just below the hook between your thumb and middle finger.

The aim is for the yarn to feed smoothly with a light, steady pull, which crocheters call tension. It will feel awkward at first. That is completely normal, and it improves with practice.

## Step 3: Crochet a chain

1. With the slip knot on your hook, yarn over.
2. Turn the hook slightly so the yarn sits in the groove near the tip (the throat), then pull it through the loop on your hook. That is one chain.
3. Repeat to make more chains. Every few stitches, move your pinching fingers up so you are always holding the work close to the hook.

For the practice swatch, make **16 chains**. To count them, look at the front of the chain: each chain is a small V shape. Do not count the slip knot or the loop on your hook.

Try to keep your chains relaxed. Very tight chains are hard to work into, so let each loop form around the wider shaft of the hook rather than the narrow tip.

## Step 4: Work single crochet into the chain

Single crochet (UK double crochet) is a short, sturdy stitch and a good first stitch to learn.

1. Skip the chain closest to the hook and insert your hook into the **second chain from the hook**. You can go under the top loop of the chain alone, or under the top loop and the bump on the back. Either is fine for now, as long as you stay consistent.
2. Yarn over and pull the yarn back through the chain. You now have 2 loops on your hook.
3. Yarn over again and pull through both loops. You have made one single crochet.
4. Insert your hook into the next chain and repeat steps 2 and 3. Continue to the end of the chain.

You should have **15 single crochet**. The chain you skipped at the start was not wasted: it lifts the yarn to the height of the first stitch.

## Step 5: Turn and work the next row

1. Turn your work over, like turning the page of a book, so the hook is back at the right-hand edge.
2. Chain 1. This is called a turning chain. For single crochet it usually does not count as a stitch.
3. Look at the top of the row. Each stitch has a V shape lying along the top edge. Insert your hook under both loops of the first V, right next to the turning chain, and work a single crochet.
4. Work one single crochet into each stitch across, including the last one at the far edge.

Repeat this row (turn, chain 1, single crochet in each stitch) until your piece is roughly square.

## Step 6: Count your stitches

Count the Vs along the top edge at the end of every row. You should have 15 each time. Do not count the turning chain or the loop on your hook.

- **More than 15?** You may have worked twice into one stitch, or into the turning chain.
- **Fewer than 15?** The first or last stitch of a row is the easiest one to miss.

A stitch marker in the first and last stitch of each row makes these edge stitches easy to find. Move the markers up as you go.

## Step 7: Fasten off

1. After your last stitch, cut the yarn, leaving a tail of about 6 in (15 cm).
2. Yarn over and pull the yarn through the loop on your hook, then keep pulling until the cut end passes all the way through.
3. Tug the tail gently to snug the loop closed.

## Step 8: Weave in the ends

1. Thread one tail onto your tapestry needle.
2. On the back of the work, pass the needle through the stitches for a couple of inches, then change direction and go back through nearby stitches. Changing direction helps stop the end from working loose.
3. Gently stretch the fabric, then trim the yarn close to the surface, taking care not to cut any stitches.

Repeat for the starting tail.

## Your practice swatch at a glance

Here is the whole swatch written the way a pattern would show it:

- Ch 16.
- **Row 1:** Sc in 2nd ch from hook and in each ch across. (15 sc)
- **Row 2:** Ch 1, turn. Sc in each st across. (15 sc)
- Repeat Row 2 until the piece is roughly square. Fasten off and weave in ends.

The number in parentheses is the stitch count you should have at the end of that row. To learn more about shorthand like this, see [how to read a crochet pattern](/blog/how-to-read-a-crochet-pattern).

## Tips

- **Slow down.** Speed comes naturally once the motions feel familiar.
- **Relax your grip.** Holding the hook or yarn tightly tends to make tight stitches and tired hands.
- **Practice chains on their own.** A few long chains are a low-pressure way to settle your tension before you work rows.
- **Undo freely.** To fix a mistake, slide the hook out and gently pull the working yarn to unravel back to the problem, then put the live loop back on your hook. Crocheters often call this "frogging".
- **Work in good light.** Seeing each stitch clearly makes everything easier.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

**The chain is too tight to work into.** Make your foundation chain with a hook one size larger, then switch back to your main hook for the rows.

**The edges slant, or the piece gets wider or narrower.** This almost always comes from adding or missing stitches at the ends of rows. Count every row, and see [why your crochet edges are uneven](/blog/fix-uneven-crochet-edges) for detailed fixes.

**The hook splits the yarn.** Insert the hook cleanly through the center of each stitch, and rotate the hook so its throat faces down as you pull through loops. A smooth, tightly twisted yarn also helps.

**The chain twists as you work Row 1.** Keep the front of the chain (the side with the Vs) facing you, and work along it in order without letting it spiral.
