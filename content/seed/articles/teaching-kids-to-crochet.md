Teaching a child to crochet can be a lovely way to spend time together, and it gives them a skill they can keep building on for years. The secret ingredient is patience: small hands need time to coordinate a hook, a strand of yarn and a whole new set of movements at once.

This guide is for parents, grandparents, teachers and anyone helping a young beginner. You'll learn how to tell when a child is ready, which tools make learning easier, how to teach the first stitches step by step, and how to keep sessions short, safe and fun.

## Signs your child may be ready

There's no single right age to start. Many children begin with finger crochet or simple chains somewhere around ages 6 to 8, but plenty start earlier or later, and that's perfectly fine. Readiness signs tend to matter more than a birthday:

- They're curious and ask to try, which is the best sign of all
- They can focus on a quiet, hands-on activity for about 10–15 minutes
- They can follow a short sequence of two or three steps
- They enjoy fine-motor activities like threading beads, lacing cards or cutting with child-safe scissors
- They can cope with a small mistake, or be gently helped through one, without giving up entirely

If a child tries and finds it frustrating, set it aside and offer again in a few months. A positive first experience is worth more than an early start.

## What you'll need

- **A larger hook:** 5.5–6.5 mm (US I-9 to K-10½) is a comfortable starting range. Bigger hooks make it easier to see what's happening and to catch the yarn. Some children find a hook with a thicker, cushioned handle easier to grip. The Craft Yarn Council's [hook size chart](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/hooks-and-needles) is handy if you need to convert between letters and millimeters.
- **Smooth, light-colored yarn:** worsted (#4) or bulky (#5) weight in a solid light shade such as cream, pale yellow or light blue, so each stitch is easy to see. Skip dark, fuzzy, very slippery, splitty or novelty yarns for now.
- **Child-safe scissors** with blunt tips
- **A blunt yarn needle** for weaving in ends
- **A few stitch markers** to mark the first and last stitch of a row
- **A small bag or box** so the project and tools stay together

Before you begin, show your child the parts of the hook. The point goes into the stitches, and the throat is the groove that catches the yarn. Loops should sit on the shaft rather than being squeezed up into the throat, which keeps stitches from getting too tight. The thumb rest gives fingers a place to steady the hook.

## Step 1: Start with finger crochet

Finger crochet uses the same chaining motion as a hook, without the tool. It's a gentle way to learn how one loop is pulled through another.

1. Make a slip knot. You can tie it for your child at first.
2. Have them hold the loop open with the thumb and finger of one hand, and hold the working yarn (the strand attached to the ball) with the other.
3. Reach through the loop, pinch the working yarn, and pull it through to make a new loop.
4. Let the old loop slip off, then gently snug up the new one, but not too tight.
5. Repeat to make a chain.

Once a long finger-crochet chain feels easy, that's often a good sign they're ready to try a hook.

## Step 2: Learn the chain with a hook

1. Put a slip knot on the hook and tighten it so it slides easily but doesn't fall off.
2. Hold the hook like a pencil or like a table knife. Either grip is fine, so let them choose what feels natural.
3. Wrap the yarn over the hook from back to front. This is called a "yarn over."
4. Catch the yarn in the throat of the hook and draw it through the loop on the hook. Tilting the hook slightly downward as you pull helps the loop slide off.
5. That's one chain. Repeat.

**About holding the yarn:** experienced crocheters often weave the yarn between their fingers to control tension, but that can be overwhelming at first. Let children hold the yarn in whatever way works for them, whether that's pinched between thumb and finger, draped over one finger, or held loosely. Tension tends to even out with practice.

Chains should be loose enough for the hook to slide back into them easily. If they're tight, encourage a more relaxed grip, or use a hook one size larger just for the chain.

## Step 3: Move on to single crochet

Single crochet (UK double crochet) makes a great first "real" stitch because it's short, sturdy and easy to see.

1. Chain 11.
2. Insert the hook into the second chain from the hook.
3. Yarn over and pull a loop through the chain. There are now two loops on the hook.
4. Yarn over and pull through both loops. That's one single crochet.
5. Work one single crochet into each remaining chain. You'll have 10 stitches.
6. At the end of the row, chain 1 and turn the work over.
7. Work one single crochet into each stitch across, starting with the very first stitch.

Point out the little "V" on top of each stitch. For now, the hook goes under both legs of the V, the front loop and the back loop together.

![Diagram of the V-shaped top of a crochet stitch with the front loop and back loop labeled](/images/diagrams/front-and-back-loops.svg)

Counting stitches at the end of each row helps catch dropped or extra stitches. A stitch marker in the first and last stitch makes the row ends easier to find. Wobbly sides are completely normal at first, and our guide to [why your crochet edges are uneven](/blog/fix-uneven-crochet-edges) explains how to tidy them up once the basics feel comfortable.

## Tips for short, patient sessions

- **Keep it brief.** Around 10–20 minutes is plenty for many beginners, especially younger children. Stop while it's still fun.
- **Sit somewhere bright** so stitches are easy to see.
- **Praise effort and persistence,** not just neat results.
- **Resist fixing every mistake.** If they're happy with a lumpy row, let it stay. The next one will likely be smoother.
- **Demonstrate slowly, then hand the hook back quickly.** Doing usually teaches more than watching.
- **Keep a practice piece** that's just for trying things out, with no pressure to finish it.

## First projects that feel like wins

Quick, visible results keep motivation high. Some good first projects:

- **Chain bracelets:** a finger- or hook-crochet chain tied into a loop. Holding two colors together gives a fun two-tone look.
- **A long chain "snake":** see how long it can grow over a week, then measure it along a hallway.
- **A small single crochet square:** about 10 stitches across, worked until it looks square. It makes a doll blanket, a coaster or a patch.
- **A bookmark:** a narrow strip of single crochet about five or six stitches wide, with a tassel at one end. Thinner yarn makes a flatter bookmark, but worsted is fine for a first try.

## Teaching a left-handed child

Left-handed crocheters usually hold the hook in the left hand and work from left to right, a mirror image of right-handed crochet. When working in rounds, they'll go clockwise rather than counterclockwise.

- **Mirror your demonstration.** If you're right-handed, sit facing the child instead of beside them, so your movements appear mirrored from their side. A small mirror can also help.
- **Let them choose.** Some left-handed people prefer to crochet right-handed. Let the child try both and go with what feels natural.
- **Look for left-handed resources.** Plenty of video tutorials are filmed left-handed. Written patterns work the same for everyone; only photos and charts will look reversed.

## Keeping it fun

- Let them choose colors. Keep the main yarn light, and add bright accents later.
- Crochet alongside them on your own project. Many families find it a calm, shared activity.
- Celebrate finishes: display the chain snake, or use the new coaster at dinner.
- Make small gifts together for friends or relatives.
- Pair crochet time with music or an audiobook.

## Safety and supervision

- Supervise scissors and yarn needles, especially with younger children, and put them away after each session.
- Teach children to return hooks to their bag rather than leaving them on chairs or the floor.
- Long chains and loose yarn can be a tangling hazard for babies and toddlers, so tidy up if younger siblings are around.

### A note on toys for babies and toddlers

If your child wants to crochet a toy for a baby or toddler, skip buttons, beads and plastic safety eyes, which could become choking hazards if they come loose. Embroider features with yarn instead, and sew all parts on securely. This is general guidance only, so inspect any item meant for a young child carefully and use your own judgment.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **Chains too tight to work into.** Fix: loosen the grip on the yarn, keep loops on the shaft rather than the throat, or chain with a larger hook.
- **Rows that grow or shrink.** This usually comes from skipping the last stitch or working twice into the first. Fix: count stitches every row and mark both ends.
- **Hook going into the wrong place.** Fix: show the "V" at the top of each stitch and practice finding it together.
- **Sessions that run too long.** Fix: stop at the first sign of frustration and end on a success.
- **Doing it for them.** Fix: guide with words, or briefly guide their hands if they're comfortable with that, then let them take over.
- **Starting with dark or fuzzy yarn.** Fix: switch to a smooth, light-colored worsted or bulky yarn.
