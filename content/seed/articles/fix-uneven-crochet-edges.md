If your rectangle is slowly turning into a trapezoid, or the sides of your scarf wobble in and out, you're not alone. Uneven edges are one of the most common frustrations when you start crocheting in rows, and the causes are few and very fixable.

This guide is for beginners working flat pieces back and forth. You'll learn how to read your piece's shape to diagnose the problem, where the first and last stitches of a row really go, and simple habits that keep your sides straight. There's also a step-by-step guide to adding a border that tidies edges at the end.

## What you'll need

- Your project, or a small practice swatch of 15 to 20 stitches
- At least two stitch markers (locking ones are easiest to move)
- Your hook, plus one a size or two larger if your foundation chain is tight
- A tapestry needle for weaving in ends

## Diagnose your edges by their shape

Start by counting the stitches in your most recent row and comparing that with the number you're supposed to have. Then look at the overall shape:

| What you see | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| Edges slant inward and the piece gets narrower | Losing stitches, usually by missing the last stitch of a row |
| Edges flare outward and the piece gets wider | Gaining stitches, usually by working into a turning chain, or the stitch at its base, when you shouldn't |
| Width wanders in and out | Gaining in some rows and losing in others |
| Stitch count is right, but the sides look loopy or gappy | Turning chains that are looser or taller than your stitches |
| Stitch count is right, but the sides pull in or pucker | Turning chains that are too tight or too short |
| Bottom edge is narrower than the rest | Foundation chain worked too tightly |

Most uneven edges come down to stitch count, and stitch count almost always goes wrong at the very beginning or end of a row.

## Understand the turning chain

When you turn your work, you make a few chains to bring the hook up to the height of the next row. This is the turning chain, and its usual length depends on the stitch:

| Stitch | Usual turning chain | Counts as a stitch? |
|---|---|---|
| Single crochet (UK double crochet) | Ch 1 | Usually no |
| Half double crochet (UK half treble crochet) | Ch 2 | Varies |
| Double crochet (UK treble crochet) | Ch 3 | Often yes |
| Treble crochet (UK double treble crochet) | Ch 4 | Often yes |

Practice varies between designers, so check your pattern's notes. Whether the turning chain counts as a stitch decides where your first and last stitches go.

### When the turning chain doesn't count

This is typical for single crochet.

1. Chain 1 and turn.
2. Work your first stitch into the very first stitch of the row, right at the base of the chain. It can hide under the chain, but don't skip it.
3. Work across, ending with a stitch in the last stitch of the row.
4. Don't work into the turning chain from the row below.

### When the turning chain counts

This is common for double crochet.

1. Chain 3 and turn. The chain stands in for your first stitch.
2. Skip the stitch at the base of the chain and work your first double crochet into the next stitch.
3. Work across to the end.
4. Work the last stitch into the top of the turning chain from the previous row (the third chain of that ch-3).

## Missing the last stitch

This is the most common reason pieces get narrower. In single crochet, the last stitch of a row tends to curl around the edge and tuck behind the turning chain, so it's easy to overlook. In double crochet, the top of the turning chain doesn't look like a normal stitch, so it often gets skipped.

Before you turn, tilt the edge toward you and look for that last stitch top. If you're using markers (see below), the answer is right in front of you.

## Adding stitches at the start (or end)

The opposite problem makes pieces wider. It usually happens in one of two ways:

- In double crochet rows where the turning chain counts, working a stitch into the base of the chain as well, which adds a stitch at the start.
- In single crochet rows, working into the chain 1 at the end of the row, which adds a stitch at the end.

Occasionally an extra stitch sneaks in mid-row, usually when two stitches end up in the same place. Counting each row catches this quickly.

## Mark the first stitch of every row

Stitch markers take the guesswork out of the end of the row. You'll need two, which leapfrog up the sides of your work.

1. Make the turning chain and the first stitch of the row. Clip a marker into the top of that first stitch, or into the top of the turning chain if it counts as a stitch.
2. Work across the row. The marker you placed on the previous row is waiting at the far end.
3. Work your last stitch into that marked stitch, then remove the marker.
4. Turn and repeat, using the marker you just removed for the new row.

In single crochet, some people also mark the last stitch of each row, because it becomes the easily hidden first stitch of the next one.

## Count every row while you're learning

Counting feels slow, but it's the fastest way to catch a problem while it's one row old instead of ten. Count after every row at first, including the turning chain only if it counts as a stitch. In wide pieces, a marker every 10 or 20 stitches makes counting easier. Once your edges stay straight, you can switch to counting every few rows.

## Keep your tension even at the edges

Many crocheters tighten up or loosen off as they turn. A turning chain pulled very tight drags the edge in; a loose one leaves loops or gaps.

- Aim for turning chains that reach the same height as the stitches beside them, without straining.
- If your double crochet edges look gappy with a chain 3, some crocheters make a chain 2 instead, or use a "chainless" starting stitch. These are personal adjustments; whatever you choose, keep your stitch counts matching the pattern.
- If the bottom edge pulls in, make the foundation chain with a hook a size or two larger, then switch back for the rest. Foundation single crochet, which makes the chain and the first row at the same time, is another, stretchier option.

Your overall tension affects the size of every stitch, too. [Crochet Gauge Explained](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide) shows how to check it with a swatch.

## Fixing a piece that's already uneven

### Rip back to the last good row

If the count is off, the most reliable fix is to pull out stitches back to the last row with the correct count. Crocheters call this "frogging". Crochet unravels easily, so it's quicker than it sounds: pull back to the right row, slip your hook into the live loop and carry on.

### Correct small errors on the next row

For a casual piece such as a dishcloth, you can make up one lost stitch by working 2 stitches into a stitch near the edge, or remove an extra one with a decrease such as sc2tog (single crochet two together). The earlier rows won't straighten, but it stops the problem growing.

### Block it

Blocking (wetting or steaming the piece and letting it dry pinned to shape) can even out mild wobbles. It won't rescue a piece that has gained or lost many stitches.

### Add a border

A round of single crochet around the whole piece hides small irregularities and gives a neat, firm frame.

1. Join your yarn at a corner.
2. Along the top, work 1 sc in each stitch. Along the bottom, work 1 sc into each chain of the foundation.
3. Along the sides, work into the ends of the rows. As a starting point, try about 1 sc per single crochet row and about 2 sc per double crochet row.
4. Work 3 sc into each corner so the border turns neatly and lies flat.
5. Join with a slip stitch to the first sc and fasten off.

If the border ripples, you've added too many stitches along that side; if it pulls in or makes the edge cup, you've added too few. Adjust and try again, since every yarn and stitch behaves a little differently.

## Tips

- **Practice on a small swatch.** Work 15 to 20 rows of 15 stitches and watch the edges; problems are easier to spot on something small.
- **Pause before every turn.** One quick look to confirm you've worked the last stitch can save rows of ripping.
- **Use good light and a lighter yarn** while learning, so stitch tops are easy to see.
- **Stick with one hook.** Switching hooks partway through changes your stitch size, which can make the edges step in or out.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **Treating every turning chain the same.** A ch 1 for single crochet and a ch 3 for double crochet are usually handled differently. Check the pattern notes for each stitch.
- **Counting the turning chain when it doesn't count, or the reverse.** This hides a missing or extra stitch. Decide which rule applies and count the same way every row.
- **Hoping the problem fixes itself.** An edge that's drifting by a stitch every row will keep drifting. Stop and recount as soon as you notice.
- **Blaming tension first.** A piece that's steadily narrowing or widening usually has a stitch-count problem, so count before anything else.
