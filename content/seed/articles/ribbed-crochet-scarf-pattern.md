A ribbed scarf is a classic first wearable, and this one uses just a single stitch: half double crochet (UK half treble crochet) worked into the back loop only. That small change turns ordinary rows into soft, stretchy ridges that look a lot like knitted ribbing. You work short rows back and forth across the width, so the scarf simply grows longer until you decide it's done.

It's a relaxing project for beginners who can chain, work half double crochet, and count stitches. If you need a quick refresher, see our guide to [the basic crochet stitches](/blog/basic-crochet-stitches). Below you'll also find the simple math for changing the width, length, and yarn.

## Pattern at a glance

- **Skill level:** Beginner
- **Finished size (approximate):** about 7 in (18 cm) wide and 60 in (152 cm) long, not including fringe. Your size will vary with yarn and tension.
- **Yarn weight:** Medium / worsted (#4). See the Craft Yarn Council's [yarn weight system](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system) if you're unsure which category your yarn is.
- **Hook:** 5.5 mm (US I-9)
- **Gauge note:** About 13 sts and 10 rows = 4 in (10 cm) in the rib pattern, measured flat without stretching. Gauge isn't critical for a scarf, but it changes the finished size and how much yarn you'll use.

## What you'll need

- Worsted-weight yarn: approximately 400–550 yd (365–500 m) for the scarf as written, plus about 25 yd (23 m) if you add fringe. These are estimates, so check the yardage on your yarn labels and buy enough from the same dye lot. Our guide to [reading a yarn label](/blog/how-to-read-a-yarn-label) shows where to find both.
- 5.5 mm (US I-9) crochet hook
- Tapestry needle
- Scissors and a tape measure
- Optional: stitch markers, and a piece of stiff cardboard about 6 in (15 cm) wide for cutting fringe

## Abbreviations (US terms)

- **ch:** chain
- **hdc:** half double crochet (UK half treble crochet)
- **BLO:** back loop only
- **st(s):** stitch(es)
- **rep:** repeat
- **[ ]:** the stitch count at the end of a row

## Pattern notes

- The scarf is worked flat in rows across its width. The ridges run from side to side, and the fabric stretches along the scarf's length.
- **Turning chain:** The ch 2 at the beginning of each row does **not** count as a stitch. Work your first hdc into the first stitch (the one right at the base of the ch-2) and your last hdc into the last hdc of the previous row. Never work into the turning chain. Every row has 22 hdc.
- Row 1 is worked into the foundation chain. From Row 2 onward, every stitch is worked in the back loop only.

### Finding the back loop

Look down at the top of any stitch and you'll see a sideways "V" made of two loops. The front loop is closer to you and the back loop is farther away. Slide your hook under the back loop only, leaving the front loop unworked. Those leftover front loops build up into the ridges.

## The pattern

Ch 24.

**Row 1:** Hdc in 3rd ch from hook (the 2 skipped ch do not count as a st) and in each ch across. [22 hdc]

**Row 2:** Ch 2 (does not count as a st), turn. Hdc BLO in each st across. [22 hdc]

**Row 3 onward:** Rep Row 2 until the scarf measures about 60 in (152 cm) from the foundation chain, measured flat, or your preferred length. At the guide gauge, that's about 150 rows. [22 hdc in each row]

Fasten off, leaving a 6 in (15 cm) tail.

### Customizing the width and length

1. **Swatch.** Ch 22, work Row 1 [20 hdc], then rep Row 2 until you have about 12 rows. Our [crochet gauge guide](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide) walks you through measuring it.
2. **Measure.** Count the stitches and rows in 4 in (10 cm) at the center of the swatch, then divide each number by 4 to get stitches per inch and rows per inch.
3. **Width.** Multiply your desired width in inches by your stitches per inch and round to the nearest whole number. That's your stitch count. Your foundation chain is the stitch count plus 2.
4. **Length.** Multiply your desired length by your rows per inch for an approximate row count, or simply keep working until the scarf is long enough.

At the guide gauge (3.25 stitches and 2.5 rows per inch):

- **6 in wide:** 6 × 3.25 = 19.5, rounded to 20 sts, so ch 22
- **8 in wide:** 8 × 3.25 = 26 sts, so ch 28
- **70 in long:** 70 × 2.5 = about 175 rows

Working in centimeters is the same idea: 13 sts per 10 cm is 1.3 sts per cm, so a 20 cm width needs 20 × 1.3 = 26 sts and a chain of 28.

**Estimating yarn for a custom size:** after about 10 in (25 cm), weigh the scarf on a kitchen scale. Multiply that weight by your target length divided by 10. For example, if your first 10 in weighs 35 g and you want 60 in, you'll need roughly 6 × 35 = 210 g, plus extra for fringe. Use the weight and yardage on your label to convert grams to yards.

## Finishing

### Weave in ends

Thread each tail onto your tapestry needle and weave it along the side edge or through the backs of the stitches, then reverse direction for a short distance so it stays secure. Avoid knots, which can work loose and show.

### Blocking

A light blocking helps the edges lie straight. Check your yarn label first. Wool and other natural fibers can be dampened, laid flat to the measurements, and left to dry without stretching the ribs. For acrylic, hold a steam iron just above the scarf without letting it touch, since direct heat can permanently flatten the yarn.

### Adding fringe (optional)

1. Wrap yarn around the 6 in (15 cm) cardboard 64 times, then cut along one edge. You'll have 64 strands about 12 in (30 cm) long, roughly 21 yd (19.5 m) of yarn.
2. Group the strands into 16 bundles of 4.
3. Fold one bundle in half. Insert your hook through a stitch at the end of the scarf, catch the fold, and pull it through to make a loop. Pull the cut ends through the loop and tug gently to tighten.
4. On each short end, place a bundle in the 1st, 4th, 7th, 10th, 13th, 16th, 19th, and 22nd stitches, so there are 8 bundles per end with one at each corner. On the foundation-chain end, work into the chain at the base of those same stitches.
5. Lay the scarf flat and trim the fringe evenly to about 5 in (13 cm).

If you changed the width, bundles land on both corners when your stitch count is one more than a multiple of 3, such as 19, 22, or 25. Otherwise, start at the corners and space the bundles as evenly as you can.

### Tassels instead of fringe

Wrap yarn about 20 times around a 5 in (13 cm) piece of cardboard. Slide a 10 in (25 cm) strand under the wraps at one edge and tie it tightly, then cut through the wraps at the opposite edge. About ¾ in (2 cm) below the tie, wind another strand around the bundle several times and knot it to form the tassel's neck. Trim the ends level and use the top ties to attach a tassel to each corner. Each tassel uses roughly 6–7 yd (5.5–6.5 m).

## Care

- Follow the care symbols on your yarn label.
- If you're unsure, hand wash in cool water, gently squeeze out the water without wringing, roll the scarf in a towel, and lay it flat to dry, easing it back to shape.
- Don't hang a wet scarf. The weight of the water can stretch the ribbing out of shape.

## Tips

- **Mark the first stitch.** Clip a stitch marker into the first hdc of each row. When you come back across, it shows exactly where the last stitch is.
- **Tidy loopy edges.** If the ch 2 leaves a loose loop at the start of each row, try ch 1 instead. It still doesn't count as a stitch, so your count stays at 22.
- **Firmer sides.** For straighter edges, work the first and last stitch of every row through both loops instead of BLO. Your count stays the same.
- **Join yarn at the edge.** Start a new ball at the beginning of a row so the ends can be woven into the side.
- **Chain loosely.** If the foundation edge ends up tighter than the rows, make the chain with a 6 mm (US J-10) hook, then switch back to 5.5 mm (US I-9) for Row 1.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **The scarf gets wider or narrower.** Stitches are being added or lost at the edges, usually by working into the turning chain or skipping the first stitch. Count every row for a while, and see our tips on [fixing uneven crochet edges](/blog/fix-uneven-crochet-edges).
- **A row without ridges.** You worked through both loops. Rip back to that row and rework it in BLO.
- **A puckered starting edge.** The foundation chain is tighter than your stitches. Start again with a larger hook for the chain only.
- **Running short of yarn.** Use the weighing method above early on, and buy extra from the same dye lot at the start.
- **Measuring while it hangs.** Gravity stretches the ribbing, so a hanging scarf looks longer than it really is. Always measure flat.

## Variations

- **Lengthwise ribs:** For ribs that run along the scarf, work it the long way. At the guide gauge, 60 in × 3.25 = 195 sts, so ch 197 and work Row 1 [195 hdc]. Rep Row 2 until the scarf is about 7 in (18 cm) wide, roughly 18 rows. Place a stitch marker every 25 chains to make the long chain easier to count.
- **Stripes:** Change color at the start of a row every few rows for bold bands.
- **Chunky and quick:** Switch to bulky yarn and a larger hook, then use the sizing math to recalculate.
- **Cowl:** Make a shorter piece and sew the two short ends together.

## Wrap it up

Once you've found the rhythm of back-loop half double crochet, this scarf becomes a soothing, repetitive make you can pick up anytime. Count often in the first few rows, measure flat, and don't worry about small wobbles; blocking and wear soften most of them. When it's finished, it makes a thoughtful handmade present, and you'll find more inspiration in our [quick crochet gift ideas](/blog/quick-crochet-gift-ideas).
