Almost every crochet project, from a simple scarf to a lacy shawl, is made from a small set of basic stitches. Once you know the chain, slip stitch, single crochet, half double crochet, double crochet and treble crochet, most patterns are simply these stitches combined in different orders.

This guide explains how to make each one, how tall they are compared with one another, how turning chains work, and where your hook goes. It is written for beginners who can already make a slip knot and a chain; if you cannot yet, start with [our first crochet lesson](/blog/how-to-crochet-for-beginners). US terms are used throughout, with UK names given alongside.

## What you'll need

- A 5 mm (US H-8) crochet hook
- Smooth, light-colored medium-weight (#4) yarn
- Scissors and a tapestry needle
- A few removable stitch markers

## How crochet stitches are built

Every basic stitch uses the same few moves:

- **Yarn over (yo):** wrap the yarn over the hook from back to front.
- **Insert the hook:** into a chain, a stitch or a space.
- **Pull up a loop:** yarn over and draw the yarn back through to the front.
- **Pull through:** yarn over and draw the yarn through some or all of the loops on the hook.

The main difference between the stitches is how many times you yarn over **before** inserting the hook. Each extra yarn over makes the stitch roughly one chain taller.

## The six basic stitches

### Chain (ch)

Yarn over and pull through the loop on your hook. Chains form the starting row (the foundation chain) of most flat projects, and they are also used for turning chains and for the open spaces in lacy patterns. The name is the same in UK terms.

### Slip stitch (sl st)

1. Insert the hook into the stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull the yarn through the stitch **and** the loop on your hook in one motion.

A slip stitch adds almost no height. It is used to join rounds, to move across the work without adding height, and to finish edges. UK patterns call it slip stitch too, sometimes abbreviated ss.

### Single crochet (sc) – UK double crochet

1. Insert the hook into the stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (2 loops on hook).
3. Yarn over and pull through both loops.

Single crochet makes a dense, sturdy fabric that is popular for toys, bags and baskets.

### Half double crochet (hdc) – UK half treble

1. Yarn over, then insert the hook into the stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (3 loops on hook).
3. Yarn over and pull through all 3 loops.

Half double crochet sits between single and double crochet in height and makes a soft, fairly solid fabric.

### Double crochet (dc) – UK treble

1. Yarn over, then insert the hook into the stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (3 loops on hook).
3. Yarn over and pull through 2 loops (2 loops on hook).
4. Yarn over and pull through the last 2 loops.

Double crochet works up quickly and gives a more open, drapey fabric.

### Treble crochet (tr) – UK double treble

Some US patterns call this stitch triple crochet.

1. Yarn over twice, then insert the hook into the stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (4 loops on hook).
3. Yarn over and pull through 2 loops (3 loops on hook).
4. Yarn over and pull through 2 loops (2 loops on hook).
5. Yarn over and pull through the last 2 loops.

Treble crochet is tall and airy, and appears often in lace and decorative edgings.

## Comparing the stitches

| Stitch (US) | Abbr. | UK name | Yarn overs before inserting hook | Approx. height | Typical turning chain | First stitch into a foundation chain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single crochet | sc | double crochet (dc) | 0 | about 1 ch | ch 1 | 2nd ch from hook |
| Half double crochet | hdc | half treble (htr) | 1 | about 2 ch | ch 2 (sometimes ch 1) | 3rd ch from hook |
| Double crochet | dc | treble (tr) | 1 | about 3 ch | ch 3 (sometimes ch 2) | 4th ch from hook |
| Treble crochet | tr | double treble (dtr) | 2 | about 4 ch | ch 4 | 5th ch from hook |

The heights are approximate, since your tension and yarn change them slightly. A slip stitch, by comparison, adds almost no height and usually needs no turning chain.

## Turning chains: do they count as a stitch?

At the start of each row you make a few chains to bring the yarn up to the height of the stitches you are about to work. Whether that turning chain counts as the first stitch of the row varies from pattern to pattern.

- **When the turning chain counts as a stitch,** skip the first stitch at the base of the chain and work your next stitch into the second stitch. At the end of the following row, work the last stitch into the top of this turning chain. This approach is traditional for double and treble crochet.
- **When the turning chain does not count,** work your first stitch into the very first stitch, right at the base of the chain, and do not work into the turning chain on the next row. This is standard for single crochet and common in many modern patterns for taller stitches too.

Most patterns say which approach they use, with a note such as "ch 3 (counts as dc)" or "ch 1 (does not count as a st)". Follow your pattern, and if it does not say, use the stitch counts given at the end of each row to check.

The same idea applies to a foundation chain. If you chain 17 and work a double crochet into the 4th chain from the hook and into each chain across, you make 14 double crochet. The 3 skipped chains usually count as the first stitch, giving 15 stitches in total.

## Where to insert the hook

The top of every stitch looks like a sideways V made of two loops: the **front loop**, closest to you, and the **back loop**, farther away.

![Diagram of the top of a crochet stitch seen as a V shape, with the front loop nearest the viewer and the back loop farther away, each one labeled](/images/diagrams/front-and-back-loops.svg)

- **Under both loops:** the standard placement whenever a pattern does not say otherwise.
- **Back loop only (BLO):** leaves the front loop as a horizontal line on the surface. Worked in rows, it creates a stretchy, ribbed texture.
- **Front loop only (FLO):** leaves the back loop unworked, forming a line on the other side of the fabric. Some patterns use those leftover loops later to add details.
- **Into a space:** some patterns ask you to work into the gap between stitches or under a loop of chains (a chain space), as in the [classic granny square](/blog/classic-granny-square-pattern).

## Reading stitches in chart form

Many patterns include a stitch chart, a drawing in which each symbol stands for one stitch.

![Crochet chart symbol key: an oval for chain, a small filled dot for slip stitch, an X or plus sign for single crochet, a T for half double crochet, a T with one diagonal slash for double crochet, and a T with two slashes for treble crochet](/images/diagrams/crochet-symbol-key.svg)

The symbols follow a simple logic. Taller stitches have taller symbols, and each slash across the stem of a T stands for one yarn over made before inserting the hook, so double crochet has one slash and treble crochet has two. The Craft Yarn Council's list of [crochet chart symbols](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/crochet-chart-symbols) covers less common stitches as well.

## Practice sampler

To feel the height differences for yourself, try this sampler. Here the turning chains do **not** count as stitches, so always work your first stitch into the first stitch of the row.

- Ch 16.
- **Row 1:** Sc in 2nd ch from hook and in each ch across. (15 sc)
- **Rows 2–3:** Ch 1, turn. Sc in each st across. (15 sc)
- **Rows 4–5:** Ch 2, turn. Hdc in each st across. (15 hdc)
- **Rows 6–7:** Ch 3, turn. Dc in each st across. (15 dc)
- **Rows 8–9:** Ch 4, turn. Tr in each st across. (15 tr)
- Fasten off and weave in ends.

Lay the sampler flat and compare each band. The edges of the taller rows may look a little loose; that is normal with this method.

## Tips

- Say the steps quietly as you work ("yarn over, insert, pull up a loop") until the rhythm becomes automatic.
- Count the loops on your hook at each stage. The numbers in the steps above are a built-in check.
- Place stitch markers in the first and last stitches of each row while you learn to recognize them.
- Work a few rows of each stitch before moving on to the next.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

**Rows gain or lose stitches.** Usually the turning chain is being counted inconsistently, or the last stitch of the row is being missed. Decide whether the turning chain counts, mark the edge stitches, and see [why your crochet edges are uneven](/blog/fix-uneven-crochet-edges) for more help.

**Taller stitches come out too short.** Check that you are making the yarn over before inserting the hook. Without it, a double crochet turns into a single crochet.

**Pulling through too many loops at once.** Pulling through all 3 loops while making a double crochet gives you a half double crochet instead. Double and treble crochet always finish 2 loops at a time.

**Mixing up pattern terms.** If a pattern says "dc" but your result looks far too short or too tall, the pattern may use the other naming system. Our guide to [US vs UK crochet terms](/blog/us-vs-uk-crochet-terms) explains how to tell.
