A yarn label, sometimes called a ball band, packs a lot of information into a small space. Once you know how to read it, it tells you how thick the yarn is, what it's made of, how much is in each skein, which hook to start with, and how to wash the finished piece.

This guide is for beginners and anyone who has stood in a yarn aisle wondering what all those numbers and symbols mean. You'll go through each part of a typical label, then work through an example of calculating how many skeins a project needs.

## What's on a typical yarn label

Layouts vary between makers and countries, and not every label includes everything. Most show some combination of:

- Yarn name and color name
- Color number and dye lot number
- Fiber content
- Weight in grams and/or ounces
- Length in yards and/or meters
- A yarn weight category symbol
- Recommended hook and/or knitting needle size
- Gauge, sometimes called tension
- Care symbols or written care instructions

## Yarn weight category (0–7)

Many labels show a small skein-shaped symbol with a number from 0 to 7. This is the yarn's category in the Craft Yarn Council's [Standard Yarn Weight System](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system). Here, "weight" means thickness, not how heavy the ball is.

| Number | Category | Common names you may see | Typical crochet hook range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Lace | Fingering, 10-count crochet thread | Steel hooks 1.4–1.6 mm, or 2.25 mm (US B-1) |
| 1 | Super Fine | Sock, fingering, baby | 2.25–3.5 mm (US B-1 to E-4) |
| 2 | Fine | Sport, baby | 3.5–4.5 mm (US E-4 to 7) |
| 3 | Light | DK, light worsted | 4.5–5.5 mm (US 7 to I-9) |
| 4 | Medium | Worsted, afghan, aran | 5.5–6.5 mm (US I-9 to K-10½) |
| 5 | Bulky | Chunky, craft, rug | 6.5–9 mm (US K-10½ to M-13) |
| 6 | Super Bulky | Super bulky, super chunky, roving | 9–15 mm (US M-13 to Q) |
| 7 | Jumbo | Jumbo, roving | 15 mm and larger (US Q and larger) |

These ranges are guidelines, not rules. Names are used loosely, and two yarns in the same category can still differ noticeably in thickness. Your pattern's gauge is what really decides your hook.

## Fiber content

The label lists what the yarn is made of, usually as percentages such as "100% cotton" or "75% acrylic, 25% wool." Fiber affects how a finished piece stretches, how warm it feels and how you can wash it. The table shows general tendencies; individual yarns vary with how they're spun and treated.

| Fiber | Stretch and shape | Warmth | Washing (general) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wool | Springy; bounces back well | Warm | Can felt with warm water and agitation; often hand wash unless labeled superwash |
| Superwash wool | Springy; may relax or grow when wet | Warm | Treated to resist felting; often machine washable on a gentle cycle, but follow the label |
| Acrylic | Some stretch; holds shape fairly well | Moderately warm | Usually machine washable; high heat can flatten it permanently |
| Cotton | Very little stretch | Cool, breathable | Usually machine washable; may shrink a little; heavy when wet |
| Alpaca | Drapey; can stretch over time | Very warm | Often hand wash and dry flat |
| Blends | A mix of their fibers' traits | Varies | Follow the label |

## Weight vs length: why yardage matters more

Most labels give both the weight of the ball, such as 100 g (3.5 oz), and its length, such as 220 yd (201 m). When you're buying enough yarn for a project, length is the number to focus on.

Different fibers and constructions have different densities. Two worsted-weight yarns might both come in 100 g balls, yet one could hold around 170 yards and another over 200. If a pattern calls for four 100 g balls of its original yarn and you buy four 100 g balls of a different one, you could run short.

Handy conversions:

- 1 ounce ≈ 28.35 grams, so 100 g ≈ 3.5 oz
- 1 yard = 0.9144 meters
- 1 meter ≈ 1.094 yards

## Recommended hook size and gauge

Many labels show a small grid symbol next to a hook or needle, along with a few numbers. This is the maker's suggested gauge, usually for a 4 × 4 inch (10 × 10 cm) square: a certain number of stitches and rows, often in single crochet (UK double crochet), with a stated hook size.

Treat this as a starting point. Your pattern's gauge always takes priority, and many crocheters need a different hook to match it. Some labels list only a knitting needle size and knitting gauge, which doesn't translate directly to crochet; in that case, use the weight category to choose a starting hook. Either way, make a swatch. [Crochet Gauge Explained](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide) shows you how.

## Color number and dye lot

- **Color name or number** identifies the shade.
- **Dye lot** (sometimes labeled "lot" or "batch") identifies the batch the yarn was dyed in.

Because yarn is dyed in batches, two batches of the same color can come out slightly different. The difference may be hard to spot in the ball but show up as a visible line across a finished piece. That's why it's wise to buy all the yarn for a project at once and check that every skein shares the same dye lot number.

If you do end up with two lots:

1. Compare them side by side in daylight first.
2. Near the changeover, alternate skeins every row or two so the shades blend gradually.
3. Or use the second lot for a separate part, such as an edging, cuffs or a border.

Some yarns are marked "no dye lot," meaning the maker aims for consistent color across batches. It's still worth comparing skeins before you start.

## Care symbols in plain words

Care symbols are small pictures that show how to wash and dry the finished item. Systems differ a little between countries, and some labels use written text instead. Here's what the common symbols usually mean:

| Symbol (described) | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Washtub | Can be washed in water. A number (degrees Celsius) or dots inside show the maximum temperature, with more dots meaning warmer. Lines under the tub suggest a gentler cycle. |
| Washtub with a hand in it | Hand wash only |
| Washtub crossed out | Do not wash in water |
| Empty triangle | Bleach may be used |
| Triangle with diagonal lines inside | Only non-chlorine bleach |
| Triangle crossed out | Do not bleach |
| Square with a circle inside | Tumble drying is allowed; dots show the heat level |
| Square with a circle, crossed out | Do not tumble dry |
| Square with a horizontal line inside | Dry flat |
| Iron with dots | Iron at low, medium or high heat, shown by one, two or three dots |
| Iron crossed out | Do not iron |
| Circle | Can be dry-cleaned; a letter inside tells the cleaner which process to use |
| Circle crossed out | Do not dry-clean |

When in doubt, follow any written instructions on the label and pick the gentlest option. Hand washing in cool water and drying flat is a cautious choice for most yarns.

## Worked example: how many skeins do you need?

Say a pattern calls for 1,050 yards (960 m) of worsted-weight yarn in your size, and the label on the yarn you've chosen says each skein holds 220 yards (201 m).

1. **Find the total length** for your size in the pattern: 1,050 yards.
2. **Find the length per skein** on the label: 220 yards.
3. **Divide** the total by the length per skein: 1,050 ÷ 220 ≈ 4.77.
4. **Round up** to a whole skein: 5 skeins. That gives you 5 × 220 = 1,100 yards, only 50 yards more than the pattern needs.
5. **Add a buffer.** Many crocheters add around 10% to cover gauge differences, swatching and small changes: 1,050 × 1.10 = 1,155 yards. Then 1,155 ÷ 220 = 5.25, which rounds up to **6 skeins** (1,320 yards).

Using meters gives the same answer: 960 ÷ 201 ≈ 4.78, so 5 skeins without a buffer; with one, 960 × 1.10 = 1,056 m, and 1,056 ÷ 201 ≈ 5.25, so 6 skeins.

There's no official buffer size, and some people simply buy one extra skein. Lean toward a bigger buffer if you're lengthening the design, tend to crochet loosely, or are using a yarn that may be hard to find later.

**If the pattern lists balls instead of yards,** multiply first. A pattern asking for 5 balls of a yarn with 125 yards each needs 5 × 125 = 625 yards in total. Then follow the steps above using your own yarn's yardage.

## Tips

- Keep one label from every project, or photograph it, so you have the care details and dye lot later.
- Check the dye lot on every skein before you leave the shop or as soon as an order arrives.
- Note the yarn weight and hook size in your pattern's margin for next time.
- Copy the fiber content and care instructions onto a card when you give a crocheted piece as a gift.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **Buying by weight instead of length.** Fix: compare yardage, not grams, especially when substituting a different yarn.
- **Mixing dye lots without noticing.** Fix: check every label before starting, and if lots differ, alternate skeins or use the odd one for an edging.
- **Treating the label's hook size as a rule.** Fix: swatch in your pattern's stitch and change hooks until your gauge matches.
- **Machine washing wool that isn't superwash.** Fix: hand wash in cool water, avoid rubbing or wringing, and dry flat.
- **Throwing the label away too soon.** Fix: tuck it into your project bag until the piece is finished.
