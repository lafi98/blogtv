Starting crochet takes surprisingly little: a hook, some yarn and a few small tools. The tricky part is knowing which hook and yarn will make learning easier, because craft shelves are full of options that suit later projects better than first lessons.

This guide is for complete beginners. It explains how hooks are sized and labeled, what the different hook styles are like, why a smooth medium-weight yarn is a sensible first choice, which small tools you will reach for constantly, and what you can comfortably skip for now.

## What you'll need

- One crochet hook: 5 mm (US H-8) or 5.5 mm (US I-9)
- One or two balls of smooth, light-colored, medium-weight (#4) yarn
- Small, sharp scissors
- A tapestry needle
- A few removable stitch markers
- A soft, flexible measuring tape

That really is all you need for your first lessons. The sections below explain each item and what to look for.

## Crochet hooks

### The parts of a hook

![Diagram of a crochet hook with its five parts labeled from tip to end: the point, the throat, the shaft, the thumb rest and the handle](/images/diagrams/crochet-hook-anatomy.svg)

- **Point:** the tip that slides into stitches.
- **Throat:** the groove behind the point that catches the yarn.
- **Shaft:** the straight section where loops sit while you work. Its diameter is the hook's size, and it sets the size of your stitches.
- **Thumb rest:** a flattened area where your thumb and finger grip the hook. The size is often printed here.
- **Handle:** the rest of the hook, which sits in your hand.

Keeping your loops on the shaft, rather than up in the narrow throat, is one of the simplest ways to make stitches a consistent size.

### Hook sizes: millimeters vs US letters

Hook size describes the diameter of the shaft. Millimeters are the most precise label and are used internationally. In the US, hooks also carry a letter and a number, such as H-8. Here are some common sizes:

| Metric size | US label |
|---|---|
| 3.5 mm | E-4 |
| 3.75 mm | F-5 |
| 4 mm | G-6 |
| 4.5 mm | 7 |
| 5 mm | H-8 |
| 5.5 mm | I-9 |
| 6 mm | J-10 |
| 6.5 mm | K-10.5 |
| 8 mm | L-11 |

Letter labels are not perfectly consistent between manufacturers, so when a pattern and a hook seem to disagree, go by the millimeter size. The Craft Yarn Council publishes a [standard hook size chart](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/hooks-and-needles) covering the full range.

Small steel hooks for crochet thread use a separate numbering system in which the number gets larger as the hook gets smaller. You will not need these to start.

### Hook materials

- **Aluminum:** smooth and very common; yarn slides along it easily.
- **Plastic:** lightweight, and often used for the largest sizes.
- **Wood or bamboo:** warm to hold, with a little natural grip. Some beginners like this because loops are less likely to slide off.
- **Steel:** used for the very small hooks needed for thread.
- **Cushioned or ergonomic handles:** a thicker, shaped grip that many people find more comfortable for longer sessions.

### Inline vs tapered heads

Look closely at the head of a hook (the point and throat) and you will notice two main shapes:

- **Inline:** the head is about the same width as the shaft and lines up with it, with a deeper, more angular throat cut into it.
- **Tapered:** the throat narrows more gradually and the head is rounder, standing out slightly from the throat.

Neither style is better. People who prefer tapered hooks often say they glide smoothly in and out of stitches, while those who prefer inline hooks often like how securely the deep throat holds the yarn. If you have the chance, hold one of each before deciding.

## Yarn

### Understanding yarn weight

In yarn, "weight" means thickness, not how much a ball weighs. A widely used standard from the Craft Yarn Council groups yarns into numbered categories:

| Number | Category | Common names |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Lace | lace, crochet thread |
| 1 | Super fine | fingering, sock |
| 2 | Fine | sport, baby |
| 3 | Light | DK, light worsted |
| 4 | Medium | worsted, aran |
| 5 | Bulky | chunky |
| 6 | Super bulky | super chunky |
| 7 | Jumbo | jumbo |

You can see the full details in the [standard yarn weight system](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system). Many yarn labels print this number inside a small yarn-skein symbol.

### Why medium (#4) yarn is a good first choice

Medium-weight yarn, often called worsted, is thick enough that each stitch is easy to see and count, but not so bulky that it feels clumsy in your hands. It works up at a comfortable pace, is widely available, and is used in many beginner patterns.

Labels on #4 yarns typically suggest a hook somewhere around 5 mm (US H-8) to 6 mm (US J-10), and the Craft Yarn Council's general guideline for this weight is 5.5–6.5 mm (US I-9 to K-10.5). Treat the hook size on your yarn label as your starting point.

### Smooth and light-colored

- **Light colors** show the shape of each stitch. Very dark yarns, such as black or navy, make stitches hard to see.
- **Smooth yarns** let you find the loops easily. Fuzzy, hairy, bumpy or velvety textures, such as mohair, eyelash, bouclé and chenille, hide stitches and are difficult to unravel.
- **Tightly twisted yarns** are less likely to split when your hook goes in.
- **A solid color** is easier to read than a multicolored one while you are learning.

The label also tells you the fiber content, suggested hook size and care instructions. Our guide to [reading a yarn label](/blog/how-to-read-a-yarn-label) explains what each symbol means.

## Small tools

### Scissors

Any small, sharp pair works. Keeping a dedicated pair with your crochet means they are always on hand when you need to cut a tail.

### Tapestry needle

Also called a yarn needle, this has a blunt tip that slides between strands without splitting them, and an eye large enough for yarn. You will use it to weave in ends and to sew pieces together.

### Stitch markers

Markers clip into a stitch to flag it: the first stitch of a row or round, the right side of your work, or every 20 chains in a long foundation chain. Choose **locking or open** markers that can be removed. Closed rings, designed to sit on knitting needles, cannot be taken out of a crochet stitch. A safety pin or a scrap of contrasting yarn works in a pinch.

### Measuring tape

A soft, flexible tape is handy for measuring finished pieces and checking gauge, which is the number of stitches and rows in a set measurement. You can learn more in [crochet gauge explained](/blog/crochet-gauge-swatch-guide).

## What not to buy yet

It is tempting to buy everything at once, but these can wait until you know what you enjoy making:

- **Large hook sets.** One hook matched to your yarn is enough to learn. A set may make sense later.
- **Specialty hooks,** such as the long hooks used for Tunisian crochet or double-ended hooks.
- **Novelty and textured yarns,** which are better saved for when you can recognize stitches by feel.
- **Fine thread and steel hooks,** which are fiddly for first lessons.
- **Blocking mats, yarn winders and similar accessories.** These are useful later but not needed for learning.
- **A big yarn stash.** Buy yarn for the project in front of you.

## Tips

- Keep the label (or a photo of it) from each yarn you use. It helps when you want more of the same yarn or need its care instructions.
- When buying yarn for a larger project, get enough from the same dye lot, since shades can vary slightly between batches.
- Store hooks in a case or pouch so their size markings do not rub off.
- If a hook feels uncomfortable, try a different handle shape or material before deciding crochet is not for you.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

**Choosing dark or fuzzy yarn for a first project.** Set it aside for now and practice with a smooth, light, medium-weight yarn instead.

**Mismatching hook and yarn.** A hook that is too small for the yarn makes stiff, tight fabric that is hard to work into, while one that is too large gives loose, gappy stitches. Start with the size on the yarn label.

**Relying on the letter alone.** Check the millimeter size, especially when following a pattern written in another country.

**Buying closed-ring stitch markers.** They cannot be removed from crochet stitches. Look for locking or open styles.
