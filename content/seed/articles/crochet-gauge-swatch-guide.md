Gauge is the link between your stitches and the finished size printed on a pattern. When your gauge matches the designer's, your sweater, hat or bag should come out close to the stated measurements. When it doesn't, small differences add up quickly across a whole piece.

This guide is for beginners and improving crocheters who want to understand gauge rather than skip it. You'll learn what gauge means, when it really matters, how to make and measure a swatch, and how to change hooks to fix it. A worked example shows exactly how much half a stitch per inch can change a finished width.

## What is gauge?

Gauge, known as tension in UK patterns, is the number of stitches and rows that fit into a set measurement, usually 4 in (10 cm), using a particular yarn, hook and stitch. A pattern might say, for example:

> 16 sc and 18 rows = 4 in (10 cm) using a 5 mm (US H-8) hook.

That means 16 single crochet (UK double crochet) stitches across and 18 rows up should each measure 4 in.

Your gauge depends on three things: the yarn, the hook and you. Everyone holds the yarn a little differently, so two people using the same yarn and hook can end up with noticeably different fabric. That's why a pattern's hook size is a starting point, not a rule.

Gauge is also specific to the stitch. A swatch of single crochet tells you little about your gauge in double crochet (UK treble crochet), so always swatch in the stitch or stitch pattern the gauge line names.

## When gauge matters (and when it doesn't)

### When it matters a lot

- **Garments** such as sweaters, cardigans and tops, where a couple of inches changes the fit
- **Fitted accessories** such as hats, mittens and socks
- **Pieces that must fit something**, such as a cushion cover made for a particular insert
- **Projects where yarn is tight.** A looser gauge uses more yarn, so you could run short.

### When it matters less

- **Scarves, wraps and blankets**, where a little bigger or smaller is usually fine (though the amount of yarn you use still changes)
- **Dishcloths and coasters**, as long as you like the fabric
- **Amigurumi**, where a firm fabric that hides the stuffing matters more than exact size

Even when size doesn't matter, a small swatch is a quick way to check that you like the fabric: not so stiff it won't bend, and not so loose that it looks holey.

## What you'll need

- The yarn you're using for the project
- The hook size from the pattern, plus the sizes one step smaller and one step larger
- A rigid ruler or a tape measure (a ruler stays straighter on a small swatch)
- A few pins or stitch markers to mark the measuring area
- A tag or note to label the swatch

## How to make a gauge swatch

1. **Read the gauge line closely.** Note the stitch, the hook size, and whether the measurement is taken before or after blocking.
2. **Chain enough for about 6 in (15 cm) of width.** Use the pattern gauge to estimate. At 16 stitches per 4 in, that's 4 stitches per inch, so 24 stitches gives roughly 6 in. For single crochet, chain 25 and work your first stitch into the 2nd chain from the hook to get 24 stitches. If the stitch pattern needs a particular multiple, round to fit it.
3. **Work in the stated stitch** until the swatch is about 6 in tall.
4. **Fasten off**, leaving a tail.
5. **Block it if the finished project will be blocked or washed** (more on this below), then let it dry completely.

Why go bigger than 4 in? The stitches along the sides and in the first and last rows are often distorted: tighter, looser or uneven. A larger swatch lets you measure a true 4 in from the middle.

## How to measure your swatch

1. Lay the swatch flat on a hard surface. Smooth it gently, but don't stretch or squash it.
2. Place your ruler horizontally across the middle, well away from the edges. Line up the zero mark with the edge of a stitch and mark the 4 in point with a pin.
3. Count the stitches between, including any half stitch. Half stitches matter, as the example below shows.
4. Turn the ruler vertically, again in the middle, and count the rows in 4 in.
5. Measure in two or three places. If the numbers differ, take an average.

To turn your count into stitches per inch, divide by 4. For example, 14 stitches in 4 in is 3.5 stitches per inch.

## Adjusting your hook size

| Your swatch has… | Which means… | Try… |
|---|---|---|
| More stitches per 4 in than the pattern | Your stitches are smaller | A larger hook |
| Fewer stitches per 4 in than the pattern | Your stitches are larger | A smaller hook |
| The same number | You've matched gauge | Starting your project |

An easy way to remember it: too many stitches, go up a hook size; too few, go down. Change one size at a time, for example from 5 mm (US H-8) to 5.5 mm (US I-9), and make a fresh swatch each time. If you need to compare metric and US sizes, the Craft Yarn Council's [hook size chart](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/hooks-and-needles) is a handy reference.

### Stitch gauge vs row gauge

You may find you can match the stitch count but not the row count. In many patterns, stitch gauge matters more, because the width is set by the number of stitches, while the length is often given as a measurement, such as "work until the piece measures 12 in". In that case you simply work more or fewer rows.

Row gauge matters more when a pattern counts rows exactly, for example for shaping. If you can't match both, prioritize stitch gauge, then measure the length often as you work. With taller stitches, row height depends partly on how high you pull up your loops, so pay attention to that too.

### Blocking the swatch

Blocking means wetting or steaming finished crochet and letting it dry in shape. Many yarns relax, grow or shrink a little when washed, so if the pattern gives gauge after blocking, or the finished item will be washed, treat your swatch the same way before measuring. Check the care symbols on the [yarn label](/blog/how-to-read-a-yarn-label) first, and be cautious with steam on synthetic yarns, since too much heat can change them permanently.

## Worked example: what half a stitch per inch does

Say a pattern for a sweater front has you work 80 stitches, with a gauge of 16 stitches = 4 in. That's 4 stitches per inch, so the front should measure 80 ÷ 4 = 20 in (51 cm) across.

Now suppose your swatch is slightly off:

| Your gauge (per 4 in) | Stitches per inch | Width of 80 stitches | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 sts (looser) | 3.5 | about 22.9 in (58 cm) | about 2.9 in wider |
| 16 sts (pattern) | 4 | 20 in (51 cm) | none |
| 18 sts (tighter) | 4.5 | about 17.8 in (45 cm) | about 2.2 in narrower |

The math is the same each time: **finished width = number of stitches ÷ your stitches per inch.** So 80 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 22.9 in, and 80 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 17.8 in.

That difference is for one piece. A sweater has a front and a back, so instead of measuring 40 in around, the body would measure about 45.7 in with the looser gauge or about 35.6 in with the tighter one. Being off by just half a stitch per inch changes the fit by several inches. Real garments also involve seams and ease (the extra room built into the fit), but the principle holds.

Notice, too, that the looser gauge adds more width than the tighter gauge takes away. Dividing by a smaller number grows the result faster than dividing by a larger number shrinks it.

## Tips

- **Swatch with the hook you'll actually use.** Hooks of the same size but a different shape or material can produce a slightly different gauge.
- **Crochet the swatch normally.** It's common to work more tightly when you're concentrating, so relax into it as you would in the real project.
- **Label your swatch** with the yarn, hook size and your stitch and row counts, and keep it for future projects.
- **Check again a few inches in.** Many people's gauge loosens or tightens as they settle into a project, so measure the actual piece too.

## Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

- **Making the swatch exactly 4 in.** The edge stitches skew the result. Make it about 6 in and measure in the middle.
- **Stretching the swatch to "fit".** Lay it flat and measure it as it naturally sits.
- **Ignoring half stitches.** Count partial stitches too; small differences grow across a wide piece.
- **Swatching in the wrong stitch.** Use the stitch named in the gauge line, not whichever one is quickest.
- **Changing hook size in the wrong direction.** Remember: too many stitches means you need a bigger hook.
